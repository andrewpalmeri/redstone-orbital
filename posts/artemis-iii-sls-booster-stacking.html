Artemis III's rocket is taking shape: SLS boosters stacked, all four engines on the core stage

NASA says both SLS solid rocket boosters are fully stacked on the mobile launcher inside the Vehicle Assembly Building and all four RS-25 engines are installed on the Artemis III core stage. Next comes a "short stack" rollout to Pad 39B for a tanking test and a crew launch-day rehearsal, while Marshall in Huntsville has finished welding the Artemis III spacer.

There’s a particular kind of quiet that settles over the Vehicle Assembly Building at Kennedy Space Center when a rocket starts to become a rocket. You can’t hear progress in a building that big. You can only see it, one tall piece at a time, rising on the mobile launcher.

On Tuesday afternoon NASA said the next Moon rocket has reached one of those moments. Both solid rocket boosters for Artemis III are now fully stacked on the mobile launcher inside the VAB, and all four RS-25 engines are installed on the Space Launch System core stage. The twin boosters will supply most of the thrust at liftoff. The four engines, veterans of a design that first flew on the space shuttle, will carry the core stage the rest of the way.

What comes next

The next step is the systems tunnel, which carries cabling and lines along the boosters. Then teams will lift the core stage and join it to the boosters, making what NASA calls a “short stack.” It’s not the full rocket, but it’s enough of one to do real work.

In the coming months that short stack will roll out of the VAB to Launch Pad 39B for a tanking test, where the launch team practices loading super-cold propellant and running the timelines it will use on launch day. NASA hasn’t given a date for that test. While the rocket is at the pad, the Artemis III astronauts will run a launch-day rehearsal: suiting up in crew quarters, riding out to the pad, climbing the mobile launcher, and walking the emergency exit route they’d use if something went wrong during the countdown. It’s the kind of drill everyone hopes is wasted effort, and nobody would skip.

The pad itself is in good shape. NASA says the Pad 39B flame deflector saw only minimal damage from the Artemis II launch, and the flame trench bricks weren’t affected, thanks to ground upgrades made after lessons learned on Artemis I in 2022.

The Huntsville part of the story

Some of this rocket comes from right here. At NASA’s Marshall Space Flight Center in Huntsville, crews have finished welding the Artemis III spacer, completing its primary structure. The spacer stands in for upper stage dimensions on this mission, and dimensional checks are underway now.

Marshall teams are also doing something quietly clever: converting two structural test articles, the Orion stage adapter and the launch vehicle stage adapter, into flight hardware. Pieces that were built to be pushed, bent and tested are being readied to actually fly. NASA says the spacer and both adapters remain on track to ship to Kennedy early next year.

Why it matters now

The timing is worth noticing. NASA is eyeing an Artemis III update around Oct. 23, though that date isn’t locked. In August, Administrator Jared Isaacman told a crowd at Cape Canaveral that Artemis III could launch as early as June 2027.

Schedules are words. Stacked boosters and installed engines are hardware. For anyone who has watched Artemis dates slide before, today’s news is the reassuring kind: not a promise, but steel and propellant tanks moving into place, built in part by people who live and work down the road from us in Huntsville.

We’ll keep watching for the short stack rollout and the Oct. 23 update.
