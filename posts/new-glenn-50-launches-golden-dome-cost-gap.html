New Glenn seeks 50 launches a year as the Senate puts Golden Dome's cost gap on the record

FAA opens comment on a Draft EA that would lift New Glenn's Cape cadence from 12 to 50 launches a year: a license ceiling, not a manifest. At SASC, CBO's $1.2T notional Golden Dome estimate met the Pentagon's $185B, and the testimony shows why they measure different things.

NASAArtemisBlueOriginNewGlennFAAGoldenDomeMissileDefenseSASCCBOSBIMDAHuntsvilleFY27

Two big numbers this morning, and neither means what the headline implies. New Glenn’s 50 launches a year is a license ceiling, not a manifest. Golden Dome’s $1.2 trillion and $185 billion aren’t competing estimates of one system; they use different yardsticks. The hearing made that gap explicit.

NASA / Launch: New Glenn asks for 50 a year

The FAA published notice Sept. 29 of a Draft Environmental Assessment for Blue Origin’s request to modify its New Glenn operator license and raise the maximum launch cadence at Space Launch Complex 36, Cape Canaveral Space Force Station, from 12 to 50 per year (Docket FAA-2026-9841). The proposal covers static fires, downrange booster landings on an ocean platform, fairing recovery and airspace closures, with no new construction. Florida Today reports the 68-page draft analyzes up to 100 static fires a year and identifies “unavoidable adverse effects,” none of them flagged as potentially significant. Comments close Oct. 30; the FAA’s virtual public meeting is Oct. 22, 5–7 p.m. CT.

The ceiling sits well above today’s reality. The pad in question is still being rebuilt after a New Glenn exploded during prelaunch testing May 28. CEO Dave Limp said Sept. 28 the company still sees “a path to launch this year.”

The NASA tie is concrete but narrow. NASA is a cooperating agency on the EA, and Administrator Jared Isaacman’s Sept. 20 workforce memo says NASA is “helping rebuild the Blue Origin pad and working their anomaly investigation” so that both lander test articles can support Artemis III in mid-2027. On the national security side, Blue Origin holds seven NSSL Phase 3 Lane 2 missions, but both non-SpaceX heavy lifters are off the manifest. Vulcan shares New Glenn’s BE-4 engine, and the Space Force said Sept. 16 it will review data from Vulcan’s commercial return flight before clearing it for military missions.

Golden Dome: the Senate put the cost gap on the record

Tuesday’s SASC Strategic Forces hearing (Chair Deb Fischer, Ranking Member Angus King) heard outside witnesses only. Their posted statements go further than day-of coverage.

Why the two numbers don’t match. CBO’s David Mosher reprised the May estimate: a notional system built to the executive order would cost about $1.2 trillion (2026 dollars) to develop, deploy and operate for 20 years. It has four interceptor layers:

  • A 7,800-satellite boost-phase SBI constellation sized for a 10-ICBM raid. That layer alone is $743 billion, about 60% of the total, because five-year orbital lives mean ~1,600 replacements a year.
  • An upper wide-area layer: two new GMD sites plus Fort Greely.
  • A lower wide-area layer: four Aegis Ashore sites.
  • 35 regional sectors, at $187 billion.

Take out the SBIs and the total drops to $448 billion. Gen. Michael Guetlein’s $185 billion covers about a decade of deployment. CBO notes it may leave out operating costs and interceptors bought through the services’ procurement accounts. They measure different things. CBO got its first (classified) Golden Dome briefing only last Friday.

Who gets protected. King pressed NIPP’s David Trachtenberg: is every American covered? “No, I’m not saying every citizen can be protected,” Trachtenberg answered. He added that deployment would come “in phases or in stages” and that he’d “rather aim high than aim low.” King also objected that the program is funded “principally through reconciliation.”

The limited-defense case. CSIS’s Tom Karako: “Weakness here is provocative.” He argued that a preferential, “home game” defense of a finite set of sites could cost “an order of magnitude less” than trillion-dollar estimates. As a comparison, he cited a 2022 CSIS cruise-missile architecture at $32.7 billion over 20 years, against a comprehensive CBO design that ran as high as $466 billion.

SBI. Full-committee Ranking Member Jack Reed asked, “if we don’t have SBI, but we spent $185 billion, have we really invested wisely?” Karako said the program would still deliver “a lot of value.” His testimony also conceded that SBI critics’ physics objections, such as the absentee ratio, “are real problems.” Karako cited Guetlein as saying SBI would be demonstrated in 2028 and deployed by 2035.

Secrecy. Fischer said secrecy keeps adversaries from “the exact contours” of the system but has fed “irresponsible speculation.” Karako urged Congress to fence funds to force release of a public document explaining Golden Dome’s strategic logic.

What’s next is money. FY26 ends today. The FY27 request put about $400 million for Golden Dome in the base budget and $17.1 billion in a reconciliation bill that hasn’t been introduced. The current CR runs through Dec. 11. Karako put it bluntly: Golden Dome “will stand or fall on FY2027 funding.” The Pentagon comptroller expects final budget decisions to land in the new calendar year.

Huntsville read. Guetlein ran programs and integration at MDA on Redstone Arsenal from 2017 to 2019. Rep. Dale Strong says North Alabama will “play a huge part.” Karako floated merging the Golden Dome office with MDA. Our analysis: an architecture weighted toward regional sectors (SM-3, SM-6, THAAD, Patriot) plays to existing Huntsville work. One weighted toward SBIs moves the money to orbit.
