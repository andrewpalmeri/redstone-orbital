The FCC just licensed a cell network in orbit: what SpaceX's 15,000-satellite direct-to-phone approval really means

On Oct. 6 the FCC authorized SpaceX to launch and operate 15,000 next-generation Starlink Mobile satellites in very low Earth orbit. They will use spectrum SpaceX is buying from EchoStar, and SpaceX won't need a carrier partner. Here's what the order actually says, how the system works, what it means for consumers, carriers and the Pentagon, and why the deadlines rest on Starship.

On Tuesday, Oct. 6, the Federal Communications Commission approved what amounts to a cellular network in orbit. In Authorization and Order DA 26-1078 (GN Docket 25-340), the FCC’s Space Bureau and Wireless Telecommunications Bureau gave SpaceX authority to launch and operate 15,000 new non-geostationary satellites built to connect directly to ordinary phones. The constellation will use spectrum SpaceX is buying from EchoStar, and SpaceX won’t need a wireless carrier’s permission to use it.

Most coverage has focused on the headline number. The more important details are in the conditions: who controls the spectrum, which waivers the FCC granted, which requests it set aside, and how quickly SpaceX has to build. This piece covers what the order says, how the system is supposed to work, and what it could mean for consumers, carriers and the national-security community.

What the FCC approved

The grant is formally titled “granted-in-part, deferred-in-part, with conditions.” Here are the key facts from the order:

  • Size and orbits. 15,000 satellites in nine shells between 326 km and 335 km, inclined from 26 degrees to a near-polar 96.87 degrees. That is very low Earth orbit, well below most of today’s Starlink broadband fleet. (FCC order; Via Satellite)
  • Services. Supplemental Coverage from Space (SCS) and Mobile Satellite Service (MSS) inside the United States; MSS and “direct-to-cell” outside it; plus Ka-, V-, E- and W-band links for backhaul and telemetry, tracking and command.
  • Phone spectrum in the U.S.:
  • Unpaired AWS-3 at 1695–1710 MHz (uplink)
  • AWS-H Block at 1915–1920 / 1995–2000 MHz
  • The 2 GHz MSS/AWS-4 band at 2000–2020 / 2180–2200 MHz (mostly downlink)
  • T-Mobile’s PCS G Block at 1910–1915 / 1990–1995 MHz, which powers today’s service

In the order’s words, that adds up to 15 MHz of new uplink “along with the 50 megahertz of D2D downlink” plus the T-Mobile lease, which “ensures SpaceX can provide meaningful two-way D2D service.” - Deadlines. Half the constellation (7,500 satellites) must be in orbit and operating by Oct. 7, 2032, and all 15,000 by Oct. 7, 2035. The license runs 15 years from the day the first satellite reaches its authorized orbit. SpaceX must post a surety bond by Nov. 4, 2026, and it has 30 days to decline the conditions. (Light Reading) - Reporting. Semi-annual reports, due Jan. 1 and July 1, must list conjunction events, avoidance maneuvers, satellites removed from service, and reentries.

FCC staff were openly enthusiastic. The order says the deployment “will reshape spectrum-based connectivity by improving competition, allowing more consumers more choices in more places.” It also predicts the system will help “initially by supplementing service availability in hard-to-reach geographic areas of the country and ultimately through increased competition in the provision of retail wireless services.”

The waiver that matters most

Under the FCC’s 2024 SCS framework, a satellite operator can use terrestrial spectrum only by leasing it from a carrier. The FCC waived that requirement because SpaceX will hold both the terrestrial and satellite licenses in the AWS-3 and AWS-H bands. In the order’s words, “Requiring SpaceX to enter into a leasing arrangement with itself would be an unnecessary regulatory burden.” PCMag and TechSpot both note that this gives SpaceX a regulatory path to sell mobile service on its own, in competition with AT&T, Verizon and its current partner, T-Mobile. (PCMag; TechSpot)

The waiver comes with limits. It can’t be transferred to a future owner of the spectrum. SpaceX also has to meet the FCC’s interim 911 routing rules “as if SpaceX were a CMRS provider.” In other words, a satellite-only service still has to route 911 calls and texts to the right dispatch center.

Several other waivers support the system design: - SpaceX can reverse the 2000–2020 MHz band to downlink across most of the U.S. The FCC accepted SpaceX’s argument that “mobile broadband uses far more downlink than uplink spectrum.” - Satellites, rather than ground base stations, can control phones transmitting in the AWS-3 band. - SpaceX gets a looser out-of-band emissions limit: −110.6 dBW/m²/MHz instead of −120 dBW/m²/MHz in the five megahertz next to its AWS-H and PCS G downlinks.

What the FCC denied, deferred or dismissed

  • Deferred: MSS uplink in 2020–2025 MHz; SpaceX’s broader request for direct-to-cell abroad across the full 1429–2690 MHz range; and Ka-band 20.2–21.2 / 30.0–31.0 GHz outside the U.S., “in light of the extensive federal use.”
  • Dismissed: Requests for the Big LEO bands (1610–1626.5 / 2483.5–2500 MHz) and L-band outside the U.S. had already been thrown out in an earlier order. That made the Globalstar and Iridium petitions against those bands moot.
  • Denied-in-part: Viasat’s petition to deny.
  • Conditioned: SpaceX can only test on the EchoStar bands until Step Two of the EchoStar deal closes, which is expected around Nov. 30, 2027. (PCMag; Via Satellite)

One detail is easy to miss. Chairman Brendan Carr’s FCC is scheduled to vote on Oct. 29 on auctioning 25 MHz in the 1675–1695 MHz and 2020–2025 MHz bands for D2D, terrestrial or hybrid use. It will also vote on modernizing the SCS rules to open up another 482 MHz for leasing. (Light Reading) Our read: the band the FCC just deferred for SpaceX is one of the bands it plans to auction, so SpaceX may have to win 2020–2025 MHz at auction rather than receive it through a license.

How it works

The spectrum comes from SpaceX’s purchase of EchoStar’s AWS-4, AWS-H Block and unpaired AWS-3 licenses, roughly 65 MHz in total. The FCC approved that deal in May (DA 26-471) on the condition of a $2.4 billion escrow tied to EchoStar’s abandoned 5G buildout obligations (SpaceNews). The deal was originally about $17 billion and grew after SpaceX added more EchoStar spectrum. Citing SpaceX’s IPO filings, Broadband Breakfast puts the total at $19.6 billion, with closing expected next year (Broadband Breakfast).

The satellites are a new generation. At Mobile World Congress in March, SpaceX’s Michael Nicolls said each “V2” Starlink Mobile satellite has a phased array five times larger than the first generation, 16 times as many beams, and “nearly 100 times the data density” (PCMag). SpaceX is targeting user speeds of up to 150 Mbps, compared with about 4 Mbps on today’s network of more than 650 direct-to-cell satellites (Light Reading). SpaceX says the satellites act as base stations under 3GPP Non-Terrestrial Network (NTN) standards, and the order says the beams will be “roughly 12 km” across. That small footprint lets SpaceX turn phone transmissions on or off in a given area, which protects federal sites and neighboring licensees.

Very low orbit is central to the design. Our analysis, not from the order: at about 330 km, the satellites are much closer to a phone’s tiny antenna than satellites at typical broadband-constellation altitudes. That helps the link budget and keeps beams small. The tradeoff is that atmospheric drag requires constant thrust and a steady replacement cycle. It also means dead satellites come down relatively quickly instead of lingering as debris.

Why it matters

This is a shift from texting to broadband. Today’s service started with satellite texting and emergency alerts. SpaceX’s own milestones show how far it has come: in October 2024 the FCC granted emergency authority to broadcast alerts and enable texting, including texts to 911, in areas hit by Hurricanes Helene and Milton (Reuters). In March, SpaceX said the service had about 10 million monthly active users and expected more than 25 million by the end of 2026 (Teslarati). Nicolls says V2 is meant to feel like a 5G network, with voice, video and data on unmodified phones, and that it will be “on most devices in the US” at launch (PCMag).

It’s global. SpaceX’s international partners are already signing up for V2. Deutsche Telekom plans service in 10 European countries starting in 2028 (TelecomTV), and KDDI is targeting the end of 2027 in Japan (TechSpot). TelecomTV notes that V2 is built around “globally harmonised S-band spectrum” (TelecomTV), which makes one global handset ecosystem possible.

It changes SpaceX’s leverage over carriers. With its own spectrum and no leasing requirement, SpaceX no longer depends on a single carrier partner. Shotwell has called next-gen Starlink Mobile “100 times better than what we’ve got right now” and says service should begin by the end of 2027 (Via Satellite). CFO Bret Johnson said last month that SpaceX could “stand up terrestrial solutions” or work with existing carriers (Broadband Breakfast).

The defense and national-security angle

The order is a commercial license, and nothing in it assigns SpaceX a military mission. Still, several facts matter to defense readers.

1. The FCC wrote federal protections into the license. The AWS-3 uplink band, 1695–1710 MHz, is shared with 47 federal earth stations, mostly weather-satellite downlinks, protected by 27 zones. SpaceX can’t let phones transmit inside any of those zones until it reaches a written coordination agreement with the federal users. It also has to coordinate through “mutual exchange of system information… consistent with national security requirements” before using a long list of federal-adjacent Ka-, V-, E- and W-band frequencies, and it must keep a 24/7 interference contact for NTIA. Our read: this shows how much of America’s prime mid-band spectrum is shared with government users, and that the FCC is relying on SpaceX’s 12 km beam control to make the sharing work.

2. The Pentagon has already asked for this capability. CNBC and Reuters reported in May that during the Iran conflict, Pentagon officials discussed having SpaceX deploy direct-to-cell service to help Iranian civilians get around government blackouts and jamming. That would mean connections “without terminals on the ground.” According to Pentagon documents Reuters reviewed, SpaceX proposed up to $500 million to set it up plus $100 million a month to run it (CNBC/Reuters). That episode came before V2 existed. It suggests that information operations and communications in denied areas are realistic uses for a network with 150 Mbps-class links to ordinary phones.

3. It sits beside the military backbone SpaceX is already building. In May the Space Force awarded SpaceX a $2.29 billion OTA agreement for the Space Data Network Backbone, formerly MILNET. That is a proliferated LEO constellation for backhaul data transport, and SpaceX has to deliver a fully operational prototype by the end of 2027. The Space Data Network supports Golden Dome and CJADC2, and the Space Force wants SDN Backbone to cover SDA’s Tranche 3 Transport Layer requirements (DefenseScoop).

Outlook (analysis, not reporting): The 15,000-satellite D2D network is commercial and runs in commercial bands. No public program puts it on the military’s tactical edge yet, and we found no reporting on Starshield-specific direct-to-device service. But if one company operates the main U.S. commercial broadband constellation, a military backbone, and a phone network in orbit, it will shape how the Pentagon thinks about resilience. Here is our view of the realistic near-term defense uses: - Emergency and backup communications for installations and first responders - Communications for allied civilian populations during a conflict - Logistics and IoT tracking where there is no terrestrial network

Smartphones and their signals are easy to spot and locate, so commercial D2D would complement protected military satcom rather than replace it. The other side of the ledger is concentration: more critical connectivity running through one vendor’s architecture, pricing and decisions.

The competitive picture

  • The carriers are hedging. On Oct. 1, AT&T, T-Mobile and Verizon formalized a joint venture that pools spectrum for D2D, led by interim CEO Paul Roth. Verizon CEO Dan Schulman argues satellite is “a complementary service… not a direct competitor” in cities and suburbs, where “95 to 98% of our revenues are” (SDxCentral). Light Reading reports that T-Mobile’s CEO put D2D at 0.0002% of network usage, and that T-Mobile and Rogers removed Starlink branding from their satellite pages (Light Reading).
  • Rival constellations. AST SpaceMobile wants to use Ligado’s spectrum. Amazon Leo has filed for a D2D system of more than 5,000 satellites and is pursuing Globalstar. Lynk and Omnispace have merged as Elveo Mobile. Both the Ligado and Globalstar deals still need FCC approval (Broadband Breakfast; Light Reading).
  • Demand is real but not settled. In a TD Cowen survey of 800 people, 34% said they would consider a SpaceX mobile service and 66% said they would not (Broadband Breakfast).

Starship sets the pace (our calculation)

The milestones are achievable only with Starship. SpaceX says Starship can carry “more than 50” V2 satellites per flight, that launches begin in mid-2027, and that about 1,200 satellites would provide “global and contiguous coverage” within six months (PCMag). Starship reached orbit for the first time on Sept. 28 and deployed 26 Starlink V3 broadband satellites (Ars Technica; Reuters).

Our math, assuming 50 satellites per flight and first launches in mid-2027:

These figures cover the D2D fleet only. They leave out replacements for satellites that decay at 330 km, and they leave out Starship’s other demands: V3 broadband, Artemis and NASA lunar work, and commercial payloads. For scale, SpaceX had launched nearly 13,000 Starlink satellites on Falcon 9 by early October, according to Jonathan McDowell’s tracking cited by TechSpot. The production pace is proven. What isn’t proven yet is Starship flying several times a month.

Risks and open questions

  • Interference fights. DIRECTV, SES, Eutelsat/OneWeb and Viasat opposed SpaceX’s request to exceed EPFD limits that protect geostationary satellites. The FCC granted the waiver for the U.S. only, required a 4-degree GSO exclusion zone, and set operations on a non-interference basis. The Space Bureau can revoke the waiver if harmful interference isn’t resolved.
  • Astronomy. The FCC said optical astronomy “fall[s] outside of our review” and relied on SpaceX’s commitments to coordinate with NSF and NASA. NRAO raised concerns about radio-astronomy interference, and DarkSky International sought an environmental review. The FCC declined, calling concerns about atmospheric effects from reentering satellites “speculative.” The American Astronomical Society also objected (TechSpot).
  • Space traffic and launch access. Blue Origin warned that a constellation this large could squeeze launch windows. The FCC accepted SpaceX’s trajectory-screening tool and coordination commitments as “currently sufficient.” The semi-annual conjunction reports will be the public record to watch.
  • The business case. Carriers argue satellites can’t match terrestrial capacity in cities. SpaceX’s answer may include small ground femtocells, an approach some in the industry have dismissed (PCMag).
  • Market concentration. One company would hold the largest LEO fleet, nationwide S-band MSS rights, and a growing role in military communications. Regulators have so far treated that as pro-competitive. Critics will test that view.

What to watch

  1. Nov. 4, 2026: SpaceX’s surety-bond deadline. The 30-day window to decline the conditions closes around the same time.
  2. Oct. 29, 2026: FCC open meeting on SCS modernization (482 MHz) and the 25 MHz D2D auction, including 2020–2025 MHz.
  3. Starship cadence: the next flights from Starbase and the first launches from LC-39A.
  4. Mid-to-late 2027: the first V2 Starlink Mobile launches.
  5. About Nov. 30, 2027: Step Two of the EchoStar deal, after which commercial service in the new bands can begin.
  6. Federal coordination: the AWS-3 protection-zone agreements, and whether any DoD user of D2D service is announced.
  7. Carrier moves: T-Mobile’s next decision on its Starlink relationship, the carrier JV’s first satellite partner, and whether any MVNO deal emerges.

Bottom line: The FCC has handed SpaceX the license, the spectrum path and the regulatory independence to build a phone network in orbit. The remaining constraints are physics, launch cadence and competition. If Starship delivers, unmodified phones getting broadband from space will become normal before the 2032 milestone, and both the carriers and the Pentagon will have to plan around it.
