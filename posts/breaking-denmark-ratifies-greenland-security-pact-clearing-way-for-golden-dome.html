BREAKING: Denmark's parliament ratifies Greenland security pact, clearing way for Golden Dome

Denmark's parliament on Thursday unanimously ratified a security agreement with the United States and Greenland, clearing the way for a Golden Dome missile defense system on the Arctic island and expanding the U.S. military role there while reaffirming Danish sovereignty and Greenlanders' right to self-determination. The executive agreement takes effect when Copenhagen notifies Washington; no U.S. Senate vote is required.

COPENHAGEN — Denmark’s parliament on Thursday unanimously ratified a security agreement with the United States and Greenland, clearing the way for a Golden Dome missile defense system on the Arctic island and expanding the U.S. military role there while formally reaffirming Danish sovereignty and Greenlanders’ right to self-determination.

The Folketing approved the pact 109 to 0, according to Danish public broadcaster DR and the U.S. ambassador to Denmark, Ken Howery. The vote was the last parliamentary step after Greenland’s Inatsisartut approved the agreement on Nov. 3. The three governments signed the deal in New York on Sept. 22, amending and supplementing a 1951 defense agreement on Greenland.

The executive agreement takes effect once Denmark’s foreign ministry notifies Washington in a diplomatic note that domestic procedures are complete, Reuters reported. It does not require approval by the U.S. Senate. The official Danish Parliament said the government can now formally notify the United States, completing ratification and making the agreement legally binding, and that President Donald Trump had the authority to conclude the pact without subsequent approval from Congress.

The deal “modernizes the United States-Denmark-Greenland defence partnership and bolsters the national security of the United States and our allies in NATO,” Secretary of State Marco Rubio said in a statement posted on X, according to Reuters. Danish Foreign Minister Lars Løkke Rasmussen said after the vote, “We have now resolved our part of the equation,” and called it “a positive development, as it removes all uncertainty surrounding Greenland and establishes a proper framework for future cooperation.” He also warned that trust with the United States had been damaged and would have to be rebuilt.

Denmark’s Defense Minister Jeppe Bruus told Reuters that the signatories will now start work on a new Greenland law to screen outside investors on security grounds and on plans for two new U.S. military bases. “I expect that the US will now also start to be more concrete on the purposes and the arrangement around these two areas,” he said. DR reported that the agreement opens the way for U.S. defense areas at Narsarsuaq in southern Greenland and Mestersvig in the northeast, and that the detailed terms must still be negotiated among the parties. Parliamentary approval, DR noted, does not allow the United States to establish new installations on its own.

The United States already maintains a military presence in northern Greenland. The U.S. Space Force’s 12th Space Warning Squadron operates an Upgraded Early Warning Radar at Pituffik Space Base, a 24-hour system used for ballistic-missile warning, missile defense, and space surveillance, according to a Space Force fact sheet.

The Greenland opening comes as Golden Dome’s leaders have been pressing Congress on cost and schedule and warning that the homeland missile threat is growing more complex. In remarks this week at a Washington Times conference, Golden Dome deputy director Marcia Holmes said China is “actively testing fractional orbital bombardment systems — FOBS,” which can be launched into low Earth orbit, “fly over the South Pole, and strike the continental United States from the south, completely bypassing our legacy radar networks.” In a separate Washington Times interview published Wednesday, Holmes put the program’s cost at $185 billion and said the team looks forward to working with Congress “to continue our progress on Golden Dome and see IOC in 28.” Senate appropriators last week wrote $9.4 billion for Golden Dome into a fiscal 2027 defense spending bill; that bill has not yet become law.

The pact is intended to ease a transatlantic crisis that began when Trump refused to rule out military or economic force to acquire the mineral-rich territory of about 57,000 people. Naaja Nathanielsen, a Greenlandic member of the Danish parliament, told Reuters that the pressure had left distrust in Greenland toward the U.S. administration. “It’s been really difficult for Greenlanders these past two years,” she said. She hoped the agreement would open a path to dialogue.

Mikkel Vedby Rasmussen, a political science professor at the University of Copenhagen, told Reuters the deal was more likely a beginning than an end, saying that with the treaty in hand the United States “will be able to demand greater influence on decisions that have security policy implications and on part of the extraction of strategic resources.” In Greenland, DR reported, the opposition party Naleraq had wanted a referendum and criticized the agreement for not delivering economic benefit to the island.
